Before we begin

Why media integrity matters

Every day you scroll past hundreds of headlines, captions, and clips — each one competing for a few seconds of your attention. Some are built to inform you. Others are built to hook you, regardless of whether it's true or fair.

This isn't a quiz. There are no trick questions and nothing to fail. You'll look at two realistic scenarios, form your own gut reaction first, and then we'll reveal exactly which techniques were at work — so the pattern sticks the next time you see it in the wild.

How it works — Read each scenario like you normally would. Answer honestly. Then reveal the analysis to compare your instincts against what's actually happening beneath the surface.
Challenge 1 · Headline Detective

The headline is a promise. Sometimes it lies.

A headline's job used to be summarizing a story. Today, on platforms paid by clicks and attention, its job is often just to get you to click — accuracy optional. Publishers know that fear, outrage, and superlatives ("SHOCKING," "PROVES," "Doctors Stunned") out-perform calm, precise language.

In everyday life this shows up as: news alerts that overstate risk, product headlines promising impossible results, and celebrity "drama" headlines built from a single anonymous source.

Why it matters — A headline shapes what you believe even if you never read the article. Studies on news consumption consistently find most shares happen from the headline alone.

Challenge 2 · Emotion Detector

Feelings travel faster than facts

Outrage, fear, envy, and heartwarming relief all make you stop scrolling and start engaging — a comment, a share, a save. Content creators and algorithms both benefit when a post makes you feel something strongly, whether or not that feeling reflects reality.

In everyday life this shows up as: captions engineered for outrage-sharing, "you won't believe what happened" teasers, and ads disguised as personal stories.

Why it matters — Recognizing when you're being emotionally steered — rather than informed — is the difference between reacting and deciding.

Three habits to carry with you

  • i.Pause before sharing anything that made you feel a strong emotion within the first five seconds — that reaction is often the point, not a side effect.
  • ii.Check whether a headline's strongest word (SHOCKING, PROVES, FURIOUS) is actually supported by a fact in the first three paragraphs.
  • iii.Ask who benefits if you click, share, or buy right now — that answer often explains the tone more than the topic does.
